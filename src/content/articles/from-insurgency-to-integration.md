On a humid evening in Sukma, a young man Keshav (name changed), once known by his underground alias Sonu, quietly returned to his village after years in the jungle. He carried no weapon—only a small bag and a government-issued certificate marking his surrender under the Surrender-cum-Rehabilitation Scheme for Left Wing Extremists. For over a decade, he had been part of the Maoist insurgency, drawn in by coercion, ideology, and the absence of opportunity. Today, he runs a small poultry unit supported through skill training under Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana and financial inclusion via Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana. His journey reflects a quiet but powerful transformation underway across India’s former “Red Corridor.”

India’s progress towards a “Naxal-Mukt Bharat” is often attributed to intensified security operations, but the deeper shift has been driven by rehabilitation policies designed by Left Wing Extremism (LWE)-affected states and reinforced by the Centre. These policies, backed by structured programmes and sustained funding, have changed the nature of counter-insurgency—from one reliant on force to one rooted in trust, dignity, and opportunity.

At the heart of this transformation lies the Surrender-cum-Rehabilitation Scheme for Left Wing Extremists, strengthened under the broader National Policy and Action Plan to Address Left Wing Extremism. The scheme offers immediate financial assistance, monthly stipends, housing support, and vocational training to surrendered cadres. The Central government has ensured its viability through funding support under the Security Related Expenditure Scheme, enabling states to implement tailored and credible rehabilitation packages.

This shift has been particularly visible in Chhattisgarh, once the epicentre of Naxal violence. In districts like Dantewada and Sukma, where state presence was once minimal, initiatives such as the Niyad Nellanar Abhiyan have complemented rehabilitation efforts. By delivering essential services—ration cards, healthcare, pensions, and banking—directly to remote villages, the state has built trust alongside offering surrender pathways. The message is clear: those who return will not only be forgiven but supported.

A similar evolution can be seen in Maharashtra, particularly in Gadchiroli. Here, the state’s rehabilitation policy, combined with infrastructure expansion under the Road Requirement Plan for LWE Areas, has created economic alternatives in previously isolated regions. Former insurgents have transitioned into livelihoods in construction, agriculture, and small enterprises, often supported by skill development initiatives. The steady stream of surrenders has not only weakened the insurgency but also disrupted its leadership structure.

In Odisha, policy innovation has played a crucial role. The revised Odisha Surrender and Rehabilitation Policy 2025 has enhanced financial incentives while tightening safeguards against misuse. Districts like Malkangiri have witnessed increased surrenders, reflecting growing confidence in the system. By learning from neighbouring states and refining its approach, Odisha has demonstrated how competitive federalism can strengthen rehabilitation outcomes.

These state-led initiatives have been amplified by broader Central schemes aimed at addressing the root causes of extremism. The Aspirational Districts Programme has accelerated improvements in health, education, and livelihoods in many LWE-affected districts. Connectivity projects such as the LWE Mobile Tower Project have reduced the physical and informational isolation of remote communities. Together, these efforts have created an ecosystem where surrender is not merely an exit from violence, but an entry into opportunity.

The results are visible and significant. The geographical spread of Naxalism has shrunk, incidents of violence have declined, and recruitment has slowed. More importantly, the social base that once sustained the insurgency has eroded, as communities increasingly align with development and stability.

The young man in Sukma is no longer an exception; he is part of a growing cohort of individuals choosing reintegration over rebellion. His story illustrates a broader lesson in governance and conflict resolution. Lasting peace is not secured by force alone, but by credible policies that restore dignity and provide alternatives.

As India moves closer to its goal of a Naxal-Mukt Bharat, the success of these rehabilitation programmes underscores a simple truth: when the state combines firmness with empathy, and security with opportunity, even the most entrenched conflicts can give way to lasting peace.
